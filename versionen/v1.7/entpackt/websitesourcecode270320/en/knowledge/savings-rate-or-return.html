Financial foundations

Savings rate or return: what moves you forward faster?

When people compare savings plans, they often focus on the expected return. Early on, a higher monthly contribution can have a much larger effect. Later, growth on the money already invested matters more. A simple model shows how that balance changes.

Simon Stark|Published 29 October 2026|Approx. 7 min read
01

Two inputs

You can plan your contribution, not your return

Your savings rate is the amount you set aside regularly. You can influence it through income, spending and priorities, though rent, living costs and a cash buffer limit what is realistic.

An assumed return is different. You can choose how to invest, what costs to accept and how much risk to take. You cannot choose the return markets will deliver. Typing 7% instead of 5% into a calculator does not make your portfolio earn more.

Change the two inputs separately when you plan. That shows what depends on your own decisions and what is merely an uncertain expectation.

02

The comparison

€100 more each month or two extra percentage points?

We start with no money invested and pay in at the end of each month. The baseline is €300 a month at a constant assumed 5% annual return. Scenario A raises the contribution to €400 and keeps the return at 5%. Scenario B keeps the contribution at €300 and assumes 7%.

After 10 years:
€300 a month at 5%: €46,309
€400 a month at 5%: €61,745
€300 a month at 7%: €51,316The higher contribution finishes about €10,430 ahead of the higher-return scenario. Amounts are rounded to whole euros.

The extra €100 a month adds €12,000 of your own money over ten years. Each payment can then grow. The higher assumed return, meanwhile, works on a small portfolio at first. Against the baseline it adds about €5,007 after ten years, while the higher contribution adds about €15,436.

These are not two guaranteed offers. Both annual returns are inputs to a model, not forecasts.

03

Over time

A larger portfolio changes the balance

After 20 years, the same model gives about €121,741 in the baseline, €162,322 with the €400 contribution and €152,261 with the 7% return assumption. The higher contribution is still ahead. After 30 years the figures are about €244,613, €326,150 and €350,836. Only then does the 7% scenario lead in this simplified model.

10 years: +€15,436 from the extra €100 a month, +€5,007 from 7% rather than 5%
20 years: +€40,580 versus +€30,520
30 years: +€81,538 versus +€106,223Each increase is measured against €300 a month at 5%. Differences between rounded end values can be €1 out because of rounding.

The reason is straightforward. An extra contribution enters every month. The assumed return difference applies to the capital built up so far. As the portfolio grows, one percentage point changes more in euro terms. On €10,000, one percentage point is €100 for a year; on €300,000, it is €3,000. Those are illustrations before costs and tax, not promised returns.

The crossover depends on every input: starting balance, time, contribution size and timing, and returns. If you already own a substantial portfolio, returns affect the result earlier. If you are just starting, you can usually influence contributions more directly.

04

Risk

A higher number in a calculator comes with uncertainty

Aiming for a higher expected return often means taking more risk. Prices can fall sharply, including early in the plan. Real markets do not follow the smooth path in our model. The final value remains uncertain even over long periods.

The example leaves out fees, taxes and inflation. Fees and taxes reduce what you keep. Inflation reduces the purchasing power of future euros. When you plan for financial independence, separate today’s purchasing power from future nominal amounts.

A high savings rate can strain everyday life too if it leaves you without a buffer. Expensive debt, near-term spending and emergency savings matter before an aggressive investment plan. German consumer advisers recommend considering goals, time horizon, risk and costs together.

05

Next steps

What to check first

Start with an amount you can save regularly without needing to sell investments after the first unexpected bill. Then test what an extra €50 or €100 a month would do to your goal. A higher contribution helps only if you can sustain it.

Use several return assumptions rather than one optimistic figure. Include weak years and a later start. If the plan works only with a high return, the goal or timeframe may be too tight.

Early on, improving a sustainable contribution is often the clearer lever. As your portfolio grows, investment costs, diversification and actual returns matter more. Withdrawals need a separate calculation: the 4% rule answers a different question from this savings comparison.
