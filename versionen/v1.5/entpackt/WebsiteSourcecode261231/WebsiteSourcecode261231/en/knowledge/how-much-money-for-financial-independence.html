FIRE basics

How much money do I need
for financial independence?

Your FIRE number depends on spending, withdrawal rate and the German framework. This article shows the rough formula and its limits.

Simon Stark|Published 15 July 2026|Approx. 7 min read
01

The basic question

What does “enough money” mean?

Financial independence, or FIRE, means that invested assets generate enough income to cover living costs without requiring work. The key input is annual spending, not income. Someone who spends less needs less capital, whatever their salary.

The rough calculation uses annual spending and a withdrawal rate: the share of assets you withdraw each year without exhausting the portfolio.

02

Core formula

Annual spending × 25 as a rule of thumb

The common rule assumes a 4% annual withdrawal rate. Because 1 / 0.04 = 25:

Required capital = annual spending × 25Example: €30,000 annual spending × 25 = €750,000 target capital.

This is a useful starting point, not a complete plan. Withdrawal rate is ultimately a risk parameter: how much can your assets support without putting the long-term plan at risk?

03

German specifics

Why the formula does not transfer one to one

  • Capital-gains tax: Investment income is taxed at 25% plus solidarity surcharge. Equity ETFs can receive a 30% partial exemption, reducing but not eliminating the effective tax.
  • State pension: Later statutory pension income can reduce the portfolio required after retirement age, but it does not fund the years before it starts.
  • Inflation: The 4% rule is intended to increase withdrawals with inflation. Ignoring that makes the result look more optimistic.
  • Health insurance: Voluntary statutory or private insurance contributions after FIRE belong in annual spending.

The formula is a good starting point. Without these adjustments it will usually understate what you need.

04

The limit of the rule

Averages are not enough

The 25-times rule assumes an average market path. Returns vary widely, and poor years shortly after retirement are particularly dangerous. One formula cannot honestly answer the probability that your money will last.

The FIRE calculator simulates thousands of possible paths and gives a success probability instead of one average. The article Retirement ruin risk explains the approach.

05

Worked example

One calculation with numbers

Suppose monthly spending is €2,500, or €30,000 per year. The rule of thumb gives €750,000. If statutory pension of €800 per month begins at 67, the portfolio requirement after that point falls because part of spending is already covered.

Your personal number depends on spending, pension entitlements, tax status and planned retirement age. There is no one figure for everyone.

06

Next step

Calculate your own number

The calculator combines saving phase, withdrawal rate and German taxes, simulates possible market paths and gives a concrete success probability.

Spending

Your FIRE number starts with an honest annual budget

The largest error often lies in spending, not the formula. Add insurance, repairs, holidays, gifts, dental care, technology and other irregular items. Separate essential costs such as housing, food and insurance from flexible spending that could be postponed in a bad market.

Annual spending = monthly spending × 12 + irregular annual spendingExample: €2,500 × 12 + €3,000 for holidays, repairs and insurance = €33,000 per year.

Time path

Your target can change over your life

FIRE does not have to be one day when all employment income drops to zero. You might work less at first, earn side income or receive a state pension later. Your portfolio then does not need to carry the same amount in every year.

  • Before leaving work: How many years must the portfolio bridge?
  • During withdrawals: Which essential costs must be securely covered?
  • After later income starts: Which costs still need to come from the portfolio?

A FIRE number stays useful only if the spending assumptions are updated regularly. Rent, insurance and lifestyle may not look the same later.

Reality check

A target is good only if it survives bad years

At €750,000, a 4% rule gives an initial annual withdrawal of €30,000. That says nothing about a weak market at the start. Selling units at low prices while withdrawing money is exactly why sequence risk matters.

Test higher spending, a lower withdrawal rate and a later retirement date. If a small change moves the plan from stable to failure, the buffer is too small. Save more, retire later or make part of spending flexible.

My order: Start with the 25-times rule, then add tax, insurance, pension entitlements and possible market paths. The rule opens the calculation; it does not finish it.
