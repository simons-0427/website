Economics

Opportunity costs
in everyday life

Every decision rules out an alternative. When you use your time for one thing, you cannot use it for another at the same time. The value of the best unchosen option is therefore a real cost, even when nobody sends you a bill.

Simon Stark|Published 18 September 2026|Approx. 11 min read
01

Definition

What opportunity costs mean

Opportunity cost is the lost benefit of the best realistic alternative you give up because of a decision. It is not every theoretical option, only the option that would probably have ranked first under the actual conditions.

The concept comes from scarcity. You have a limited amount of money, time, attention or capacity. Once you use that resource for one option, you partly exclude the others. The benefit of the best excluded option is the economic cost.

Opportunity cost = benefit of the best unchosen alternativeOnly a realistic alternative that was genuinely available counts.

These costs are normally not paid and appear on no invoice. They still show what a decision costs economically. A free activity can have a high opportunity cost if it uses a lot of time.

02

Price and cost

Why the price does not show the full cost

The direct cost is visible at checkout: a product costs €50, a ticket €30 and a course may cost nothing. Opportunity cost asks what benefit the same money or time could have produced elsewhere.

A free video costs no money, but 45 minutes spent watching it cannot be used for learning, work, exercise or rest. The opportunity cost is the benefit of the best of those alternatives. The same €50 or two hours can therefore have different value for different people and on different days.

Opportunity cost depends on the situation: available money, time pressure, realistic alternatives and the value assigned to them. Price is usually the same for all buyers; opportunity cost is a changing assessment of what was given up.

03

Time

Time is often the most important scarce resource

Money can sometimes be earned again. A past hour does not return. That makes opportunity cost particularly useful for time decisions. Ask not only “What am I doing now?” but also “What else could I use this time for?”

Suppose you have four free hours on Saturday and could realistically work for €14 per hour. Choosing a film night gives up a gross alternative value of €56. That does not make the film night a bad decision: rest and social time may be worth more to you. The €56 simply shows what you give up.

An hourly wage is a useful estimate only if the work was actually available and you would have accepted it. A made-up job is not a real alternative. The best alternative may be sleep or study. The concept helps expose hidden time costs such as long commutes, unnecessary coordination, endless scrolling and tasks that planning could shorten.

04

Money

What spending changes in your wealth

With money decisions, consider more than what leaves the account today. If you spend €1,000, you cannot save, invest or use that money to repay debt at the same time. The opportunity cost is the lost financial benefit of the best option.

Assume the realistic alternative is an investment with an expected average return of 5% per year. After five years, before costs and taxes and with no guarantee:

€1,000 × 1.055 ≈ €1,276Forgone growth relative to spending today: about €276.

The €276 is not a promise. Returns are uncertain and costs and taxes depend on the investment. The purchased item also provides a benefit: a tool that saves time or earns money belongs on the other side of the comparison.

05

Everyday life

Where the concept appears constantly

You do not need to convert every small choice into euros. Often it is enough to see the relevant alternative:

  • Cook or order: Cooking saves money but takes time; ordering does the opposite.
  • Car or bus: A car can save time at a higher direct cost, while the bus may let you read.
  • Overtime or free time: More income competes with rest, relationships and other tasks.
  • Cheap or fast: The cheaper option is not cheaper if it consumes time that could create more value elsewhere.
  • Subscription or one-off purchase: A subscription may be cheaper per use while tying up money for a service you rarely use.

There is rarely one objectively correct answer. Opportunity costs simply make the trade visible: you gain the chosen benefit and give up the best alternative. Whether that trade is sensible depends on your goals and situation.

06

Common errors

What often goes wrong

Adding every alternative together. Only the best realistic alternative counts, not the combined value of everything you did not choose.

Treating uncertainty as certainty. An expected 5% return is not guaranteed, and a side job counts only if you could really take it. The more uncertain the option, the more cautiously value it.

Valuing every free hour at your wage. Rest has its own benefit. The alternative to resting is not automatically 60 minutes of work.

Including sunk costs. Money already spent usually does not determine the next decision. Compare the benefits of staying or leaving from now on.

Counting direct cost twice. The €1,000 purchase price and the €276 forgone growth describe different points in the same decision. Do not add them into a fake exact total without a clear question.

07

Marginal unit

The next hour or euro usually matters most

You rarely need to re-evaluate your entire life. Usually the decision is small: another half hour of study, another €20, another workout or another task.

Compare the additional benefit of that next unit with the best alternative. The first study hour before an exam may help a lot; the third may help less while sleep becomes more valuable. The opportunity cost of the third hour includes the poorer concentration tomorrow.

The same applies to money. Does the next euro create more value in an investment, a new device, a holiday or debt repayment? Marginal thinking prevents averages from hiding the concrete decision. Time already spent is gone; it does not justify spending the next hour.

08

A practical process

How to examine a decision

For important choices, five questions are often enough:

1. What decision am I making? 2. Which resource is scarce: money, time, attention or capacity? 3. Which alternatives are genuinely possible right now? 4. Which is the best unchosen alternative? 5. Is the chosen option's benefit greater than the benefit I give up?If a number cannot be estimated honestly, describe the direction instead of inventing precision.

Compare money over the same period: €1,000 today and a possible investment value in five years are not the same quantity. For time, check whether the hourly value fits the situation. For personal choices, accept that rest, relationships and joy cannot be fully priced.

The aim is not to turn every decision into a spreadsheet. It is to remember the alternative that the decision actually removes.

09

Worked example

Cook or order?

A home-cooked meal costs €8 and takes 45 minutes. Ordering costs €18 and takes 15 minutes. Cooking is cheaper at first glance, but it uses 30 extra minutes. If you could realistically tutor for €15 per hour during that half hour, its opportunity cost is €7.50.

Direct cost: €8
Opportunity cost of the extra 30 minutes: 0.5 × €15 = €7.50
Total comparison value: €8 + €7.50 = €15.50Ordering costs €18. Under these assumptions, cooking is €2.50 cheaper.

The result changes if tutoring is unavailable, the half hour is needed for recovery or cooking itself gives you more value. Opportunity cost is not a permanent price for a meal; it is a calculation for a specific situation.

10

In short

The best alternative belongs in every decision

Opportunity cost is the benefit of the best realistic alternative you give up. It can be measured in euros, but not always. With time, rest and relationships, a qualitative comparison may be more honest.

Before spending money, an hour or your attention, briefly ask what else you could do with it. You do not always have to choose differently, but you should know the trade you are making.
