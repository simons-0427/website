FIRE basics

The 4% rule
explained simply

The 4% rule is the best-known rule of thumb in the FIRE movement. This article explains where it comes from, how it works and where its limits begin.

Simon Stark|Published 4 June 2026|Approx. 7 min read
01

Origin

Where the rule comes from

The 4% rule goes back to the Trinity Study from 1998, together with earlier work by financial adviser William Bengen. Both looked at historical US market data and asked which annual withdrawal rate could support a portfolio for 30 years without running out. A withdrawal rate of roughly 4% worked across most historical periods in that dataset.

02

How it works

How to apply the rule

In the first year, withdraw 4% of your starting portfolio. In every following year, increase that original amount with inflation so that your purchasing power stays broadly constant. Portfolio performance does not reset the withdrawal amount.

Annual withdrawal in year 1 = starting capital × 4% Example: €750,000 × 4% = €30,000 in the first year. Later withdrawals are adjusted for inflation.

The familiar reverse calculation is annual spending × 25 = the required portfolio. For a full worked example, see How much money do I need for financial independence?

03

The downside

Why 4% is not automatically safe

The Trinity Study is based on historical US data from the twentieth century. Past returns are not a guarantee for the future. Two issues matter in particular:

  • Sequence-of-returns risk: Over 30 years, the order of returns matters more than their average. A crash in the first years of retirement hurts far more than the same crash ten years later because withdrawals have already reduced the portfolio.
  • Taxes: The original study does not model capital-gains tax. In Germany, withholding tax reduces what is actually available to spend, so 4% gross is not 4% net.
04

Putting it in context

A useful starting point, not a final answer

The 4% rule is a useful first reference, but a static number cannot capture your personal risk. It does not tell you how likely your own portfolio is to last. It only says that this rate worked on average in a particular historical sample. A serious plan needs many possible market paths, not one number.

That is why a simulation based on your spending, age and portfolio mix is more helpful than treating 4% as a promise. The related article Retirement ruin risk explains that approach.

What the number means

4% is a starting withdrawal, not a salary

The 4% applies to the portfolio at the beginning of the withdrawal phase. With a portfolio of €750,000, that is €30,000 in year one. In year two you do not simply take 4% of the then-current balance; the original amount is first adjusted for inflation.

Your portfolio does not pay a guaranteed interest rate. You sell units, receive distributions and live with the portfolio's path. The rule describes a historical withdrawal strategy under specific assumptions, not a secure return.

Starting withdrawal = portfolio at the start of retirement × 0.04 €750,000 × 0.04 = €30,000 in the first year. The classic version adjusts later withdrawals for inflation.

Adjustments

Flexible withdrawals can reduce risk

A fixed withdrawal is easy to plan around, but it becomes demanding when the portfolio falls immediately after retirement. You then take the same inflation-adjusted amount from a smaller capital base. A flexible strategy can temporarily cut spending or postpone larger purchases.

In practice, protect essential spending and tie travel, new technology or other variable costs to portfolio performance. Spend more in strong years and hold back in weak years. Decide the rules in advance; changing them during a crash is much harder than following a limit you chose while saving.

Personal framework

The right rate depends on your plan

A 30-year horizon is not equally relevant to everyone. Someone stopping work at 40 needs a different plan from someone leaving work shortly before the statutory retirement age. Later pension income, rental income or part-time work also changes what the portfolio must cover.

  • Long horizon: The longer the money must last, the more cautiously you should treat the rate.
  • Additional income: A later state pension can reduce the portfolio requirement, but it does not fund the years before it starts.
  • Tax and insurance: Include the amounts that must actually reach you after tax and health insurance.
  • Portfolio: Equity allocation, bonds, costs and rebalancing all affect the path. The 4% is not independent of the allocation.

The rule is useful as a starting point, not as the end of the analysis. The real question is: how often does my money last under my conditions?

What else belongs in the model

Costs, portfolio and lifespan belong together

Fees, taxes, spreads and other friction reduce what is available for spending. Two portfolios with the same gross return can therefore support different withdrawals. Allocation matters too: a high equity share may offer more long-term growth but larger temporary losses, while a larger safe-asset share can reduce volatility but may struggle to preserve purchasing power over a very long horizon.

When you use the 4% rule, always record the horizon, portfolio and spending level it refers to. Without that frame, the number is too rough for a real decision.
