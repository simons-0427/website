Property investment

Evaluating property as an investment:
the key metrics

The purchase price alone says little about a property investment. These metrics show how the property actually works as an investment.

Simon Stark|Published 7 July 2026|Approx. 6 min read
01

Gross rental yield

A quick first look

Gross rental yield = annual net rent / purchase price × 100Example: €12,000 / €300,000 × 100 = 4.0%

Gross yield is useful for a quick comparison between properties, but it ignores purchase costs, operating costs and financing. It is not enough for a decision.

02

Net rental yield

Rental yield after costs

Net rental yield = (annual net rent − non-recoverable costs) / (purchase price + purchase costs) × 100Administration, maintenance, vacancy risk and purchase costs are included.

Net yield is often one to three percentage points below gross yield because it includes the costs that actually occur. The companion article Planning purchase costs and financing correctly explains which costs belong in the calculation.

03

Cash flow & return on equity

What remains each month

Rental yields look at the property without financing. In practice, monthly cash flow matters: rent minus operating costs minus interest and principal payments. Positive cash flow means the property supports itself; negative cash flow requires a contribution.

Return on equity (ROE) relates the result to the equity invested. With a high debt share, ROE can be well above the net rental yield, but the monthly obligation also rises. A property can show an attractive ROE on paper and still cost money every month.

04

Price multiplier

A fast market comparison

Price multiplier = purchase price / annual net rentExample: €300,000 / €12,000 = a multiplier of 25

The multiplier says how many annual rents equal the purchase price. A lower number can indicate a cheaper valuation relative to rent, but it does not replace a multi-year economic analysis. Future income, costs and financing still matter.

Worked example

From purchase price to real yield

Take the example property at €300,000 with €12,000 annual net rent. Gross yield is 4%. Add €30,000 purchase costs and €2,400 of annual non-recoverable costs, administration and reserves:

Simplified net income = €12,000 − €2,400 = €9,600 €9,600 / €330,000 total investment × 100 ≈ 2.9% before financing and taxThis is an illustration, not a market assumption. Net yield falls because costs that gross yield hides are now visible.

Financing then changes the view. Separate property return, monthly cash flow and return on equity. Check that you can carry payments, reserves and vacancy even if rent does not rise.

Before buying

Metrics do not replace property due diligence

A spreadsheet can show that a property looks interesting. It cannot tell you whether it will remain lettable, whether major repairs are coming or whether the assumed rent is realistic. Review location, condition, lease, owners' association and planned measures as well.

A high gross yield may come from a low price, an unrealistic rent, a maintenance backlog or a difficult location. The metric is a filter, not proof. My order is: estimate rent and costs realistically, model financing, then look at ROE. If the result works only under optimistic assumptions, put that openly in the bear case.

Time and sale

Annual yield does not tell the whole story

A property can have negative cash flow in year one and still build equity through repayment, rent growth and a change in property value. The reverse is also possible: positive first-year cash flow can hide a later renovation or expensive refinancing. Compare the full holding period, including starting equity, cash flow, repayment, reserves, selling costs and net sale proceeds.

Holding-period result = cash flow + principal repayment + net sale proceeds − equity investedTaxes, fees and changes in value need to be modelled separately in a complete calculation.

Do not put operating costs, reserves and principal repayment into one undifferentiated “cost” line. An expense reduces wealth; a reserve is planned for a future expense; principal reduces debt while reducing liquidity. That distinction makes the result easier to understand.
