Economics

What is the
minimum principle?

Set the result you need. Then find the path that reaches it with the smallest necessary input. The result stays fixed; effort is reduced as far as the rules allow.

Simon Stark|Published 5 August 2026|Approx. 10 min read
01

Definition

What the minimum principle is

The minimum principle is: achieve a defined result with the least input. The result may be a quantity, delivery volume, minimum quality, service level or legal standard. Input is typically money, time, material, machine hours or staff.

The direction matters: define the target first, then ask how little input still achieves it. Among all paths that reach the target, choose the one with the lowest effort.

Minimum principle = fixed result + minimum permitted inputLess output is not an optimisation if the target is no longer met.
02

Two quantities

Separate result and input

Output is what must exist at the end: 1,000 finished parts, 50 answered customer requests per day, a building meeting a standard or a daily ration with defined minimums. Input is what you consume to get there: euros, hours, fuel or steel. That is the quantity to minimise.

Taste, image and comfort are either part of the result, a constraint or not an optimisation target. Once that distinction is clear, the question becomes precise: given the result, what is the smallest sensible input?

03

Example

Deliver 1,000 units

A company must deliver 1,000 units. It can choose expensive or cheaper machines, more manual work, other suppliers or different bills of materials. Plan A costs €12,000 and plan B €9,500; both meet quantity and quality, so B wins. Plan C costs €8,000 but delivers only 900 units or fails quality, so it is not a solution.

Key point: Cheap and non-compliant is not an admissible plan.
04

Why it matters

Scarce resources make the principle useful

Money, time, raw materials and capacity are scarce. Where the result is mandatory, spending more without changing the result has no benefit. The same logic appears in production, logistics, purchasing, projects and public services: target fixed, input as small as possible, but never below what the target requires.

  • Production: fixed quantity at the lowest total effort or unit cost.
  • Logistics: fixed stops or delivery windows with minimum time or fuel.
  • Purchasing: fixed quantities and quality rules at the cheapest basket.
  • Projects: defined deliverable with minimum resources while scope and quality hold.
05

Requirements

What must be measurable

You need a clear target such as 1,000 units, at least 95% availability or at least 160 g protein per day. You also need a measurable input such as euros, hours, kilograms or kWh. Finally, define which alternatives, capacities, quality levels and legal limits are allowed.

Constraints can be lower bounds, such as minimum performance, or upper bounds, such as maximum risk, capacity, calories or budget. The minimum principle chooses the lowest-input plan inside that permitted space.

06

Common errors

What often goes wrong

  • “Minimum” as permission for poor quality: A cheap plan that breaks the standard is not admissible.
  • Wrong target: A badly defined quantity or minimum optimises in the wrong direction.
  • Incomplete costs: Excluding logistics, waste, rework or time minimises the wrong input.
  • Too narrow a pool: If only expensive alternatives are allowed, the minimum within that pool is still expensive. That is a selection problem, not a failure of the principle.
07

Admissible vs. optimal

Many plans can work; one is cheapest

Every plan that meets the target and constraints is admissible. Among those, at least one has the lowest input. That is the plan the minimum principle seeks. “Admissible” means the target is met and constraints hold; “optimal” here means no other admissible plan is cheaper.

08

Constraints

What narrows the solution space

Without constraints, the minimum would often be trivial and useless: do nothing, spend zero and produce zero. Minimum quantities, maximum capacities, technical dependencies and allowed alternatives force the target and the real boundaries.

The more meaningful the constraints, the clearer the question: which point inside the permitted space has the smallest input?

09

Formal view

Turn it into a calculation

minimise input = Σ (costi × quantityi)
subject to minimum performance, capacities, quality limits and quantityi ≥ 0Each alternative has a cost and contributions to the result. Quantities are the decisions; constraints enforce the target.

10

In short

The rule in one sentence

Define the result. Define the rules. Among all paths that achieve both, choose the one with the smallest necessary input. That is the minimum principle: not less result, but less effort for the same result.
