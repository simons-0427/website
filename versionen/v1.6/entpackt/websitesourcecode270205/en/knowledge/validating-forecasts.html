Validating forecasts: can your model predict new data?

A model may fit old data well and still miss next month. A forecast earns trust only when it works on later values that were unknown when the prediction was made. A small cash-flow example shows how to test that.

Simon Stark|Published 12 November 2026|Approx. 8 min read
01

Before testing

Define the forecast date and target

Suppose you want to predict next month’s cash flow from a rented flat. Each forecast needs a date: when was it made, and which figures were known then? The actual cash flow for the target month cannot appear in the model or in a precomputed input.

Split the months in time order. The first twelve form the training period here, with the next four held back for testing. Fit the model using only the first part. Mixing old and new months at random could let future information leak into earlier predictions.

Data preparation counts too. Averages, scaling and the choice of input variables must be learned using only the training data available at that point. The entire route to the forecast has to respect time.

02

A simple benchmark

Does the model beat the last known month?

Our baseline uses the previous month’s cash flow. Each test prediction is made after the preceding month has closed. The final training month was €1,000. The model figures are invented too; their purpose is to show the test.

Month 1: Actual €1,200 | Model €1,100 | Previous month €1,000
Month 2: Actual €1,100 | Model €1,150 | Previous month €1,200
Month 3: Actual €900 | Model €850 | Previous month €1,100
Month 4: Actual −€1,000 | Model €800 | Previous month €900Four consecutive one-month-ahead forecasts. The previous-month baseline uses only the last completed month. All values are invented.

The model is closer in the first three months. In month four it predicts €800 of surplus when cash flow is actually minus €1,000. That is the month a liquidity plan most needs to catch. An average score can hide it.

03

Error in euros

MAE keeps the original unit

Mean absolute error, or MAE, takes the size of each forecast error without its sign and averages those values. The model misses by €100, €50, €50 and €1,800. The baseline misses by €200, €100, €200 and €1,900.

Model: (€100 + €50 + €50 + €1,800) ÷ 4 = €500 MAE
Previous month: (€200 + €100 + €200 + €1,900) ÷ 4 = €600 MAELower is better for this measure. MAE is in the same unit as cash flow. Four months are far too few to promise stable performance.

By this measure, the model wins. That is not enough for a decision: its largest miss is €1,800, and it gets the sign wrong. If the question is whether cash reserves will cover a shortfall, show the largest error and every negative cash-flow month as well.

04

Percentage errors

Why MAPE struggles near zero

Mean absolute percentage error, or MAPE, divides each absolute error by the size of the actual value before averaging. An error of €100 against an actual €1,000 is 10%. The same €100 error against an actual €10 is 1,000%. At zero, the usual division is undefined.

Actual €10, forecast €110: |110 − 10| ÷ |10| × 100 = 1,000%
Actual €0, forecast €100: percentage error undefinedThe miss is €100 in both cases. MAPE for cash-flow months near zero or with sign changes can therefore be hard to interpret.

For a liquidity decision, the error in euros is often easier to use. MAPE may add context when actual values stay well away from zero and percentages are meaningful. The right measure depends on the decision.

05

Bad months

Inspect the distribution of errors

Separate test months with positive and negative cash flow. Show each error in time order and flag occasions when the model missed a deficit. In our example, reporting only “€500 MAE” while hiding the fourth month would mislead the reader.

Where possible, compare different periods, market conditions and forecast horizons. A good next-month forecast is not automatically good six months ahead. With only four test months, you can hardly judge rare failures.

When forecasts inform decisions, show plausible ranges or adverse scenarios alongside the point estimate. One number cannot describe the range of possible errors.

06

A fair test

Keep the test period out of model tuning

A common mistake is to inspect the same four test months repeatedly and keep changing the model until it performs well on them. Those months have then become part of development. A final assessment needs later data left untouched during tuning.

With longer series, move the forecast origin forward repeatedly: train on earlier observations, forecast the next period, record the error and then advance. This is often called rolling time-series validation. Each forecast must reproduce the information available on its original date.

Before keeping a complex method, compare it with a simple baseline. If it performs little better, its extra complexity has not shown a useful gain. Check data quality and the limits of financial backtests too.
