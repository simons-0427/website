Economic concepts

Inflation and purchasing power: why your own basket matters

Germany’s official inflation rate measures the average price change for a weighted basket. Your spending may look different. If rent and food take up a large share of your budget, your own purchasing power can move differently from the headline figure.

Simon Stark|Published 21 October 2026|Approx. 7 min read
01

The average

What the official inflation rate measures

Germany’s Federal Statistical Office measures the prices of goods and services bought by private households with the consumer price index. Each category enters the calculation according to its share of spending. The result is an average across households, not a statement for every individual bank account.

If you do not own a car, higher fuel prices do not affect you directly. If rent takes a large share of your budget, its development matters more to you. That is why Destatis offers a personal inflation calculator.

The official rate remains a useful benchmark. To understand what your own money can buy, you also need to look at where you spend it.

02

Price level

Lower inflation does not restore old prices

An inflation rate measures the change in a price index against a comparison period. If the rate falls from 10% to 2%, prices in this example rise more slowly. They do not automatically fall.

Price index at first: 100
After a 10% rise: 110
After another 2% rise: 112.2The price level is now 12.2% above the starting point. The second rate is lower, but the index still rises.

A one-off price jump can therefore remain in your budget for years after the current inflation rate has eased. Prices would have to decline for the index to return to its earlier level.

03

Your basket

Start with your own spending weights

Take a completed twelve-month period and divide your consumption spending into a few groups. In the example, housing and energy make up 40%, food 25%, transport 15% and other goods and services 20%. Together, they add up to 100%.

Each group needs a price change for the same period. We assume 6% for housing and energy, 5% for food, minus 2% for transport and 2% for the rest. These values are invented to make the calculation clear.

40% × 6% + 25% × 5% + 15% × (−2%) + 20% × 2% = 3.75%This simplified personal basket becomes 3.75% more expensive. The weights come from spending in the starting period.

It is a weighted approximation, not a fully constructed price index with strict controls for comparable products and quality.

04

Purchasing power

What a pay rise buys

If your disposable income rises by 3% while your example basket costs 3.75% more, the higher income buys slightly less of the same basket. The exact comparison is 1.03 divided by 1.0375, rather than simply subtracting 3.75 from 3.

1.03 ÷ 1.0375 − 1 ≈ −0.72%Simplified change in purchasing power for this basket. Taxes, changing quantities and other income are left out.

Destatis calculates Germany’s real wage index using the official consumer price index. Your personal estimate can differ because your spending weights differ.

05

Limits

A higher bill is not always a higher price

A higher electricity bill may mean the price per kilowatt-hour rose. You may also have used more electricity. To measure a price change, compare the same quantity and a comparable service. Moving from a cheaper product to a premium one changes your spending, but does not by itself measure inflation.

For a homeowner, the mortgage payment matters for the budget. Repaying principal is not a consumer price in the official basket. Track it separately in your cash-flow plan. Taxes and saving are not simply consumer goods either.

A small homemade basket also struggles with quality changes and new products. It is useful for personal budgeting, but cannot replace official price statistics.

06

Use it

Keep assumptions consistent in your plan

Record your spending weights and the period first. Then use price series for matching product groups or compare identical products and contracts. Combine each weight with its price change and revisit the assumptions regularly.

This matters over long planning periods. A fixed nominal saving amount buys less when prices rise. When planning for financial independence or ETF withdrawals, keep needs in today’s euros separate from needs in future euros.

To apply the example to your household, start with four to six major expense groups. More decimal places will not help while prices and quantities remain uncertain.
